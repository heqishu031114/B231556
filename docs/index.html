Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic provides a natural experiment for assessing how a major health-system disruption affected community analgesic prescribing in Scotland. Scotland’s first confirmed COVID-19 case on 1 March 2020 was followed by a national lockdown on 24 March 2020, with rapid expansion of remote consultations and postponement of elective care, and legal restrictions were largely removed by 18 April 2022 (Scottish COVID-19 Inquiry, 2025). COVID-19 also changed the population’s symptom burden. Headache, dyspnoea, myalgia, musculoskeletal pain and persistent cough were common features of both acute infection and post-viral illness (Evcik, 2023; Nalbandian et al., 2021). As a result, analgesics, from first-line agents such as paracetamol to stronger options such as co-codamol were frequently used to manage moderate to severe symptoms. These clinical patterns provide context for interpreting prescribing changes across the pandemic. Using Public Health Scotland “Prescriptions in the Community” data, this analysis examines how analgesic prescribing evolved across three non-overlapping 26-month windows:

  • Before COVID: January 2018 – February 2020

  • During COVID: March 2020 – April 2022

  • After COVID: May 2022 – June 2024

The primary outcome is the number of paid items for analgesics (BNF section 04.07), providing a consistent indicator of prescribing volume over time (Open Prescribing, 2025; Public Health Scotland, 2025). The analysis addresses three questions:

  1. Did the overall analgesic prescribing volume change across the three periods?

  2. Which specific analgesic products dominated prescribing before, during and after COVID-19, and how did their shares change?

  3. Were any observed changes brief responses around the onset of the pandemic, or did they develop into longer-term shifts in practice?

To answer these questions, the report summarises the top-10 most prescribed analgesics by volume in each period, analyses changes in their percentage share using a heatmap, and evaluates monthly time-series for both total analgesic items and the union of top-10 analgesics.

Results

Table 1 and Figure 1 together show that Scottish community analgesic prescribing was dominated by a stable group of medicines across all three periods. Paracetamol 500mg tablet and co-codamol 30/500mg tablet remain the top two items throughout, jointly accounting for around 40-43% of all analgesics. Their prominence reflects established clinical roles: paracetamol as a first-line analgesic, and co-codamol 30/500mg as the routine step-up option when monotherapy is insufficient for moderate to severe pain or distressing cough—symptoms common in acute and post-viral COVID-19 illness (Chauhan, 2020). Their consistently high ranking likely reflects both routine pain management and COVID-related symptom burden.

Tramadol 50mg capsule and dihydrocodeine 30mg tablet also remain high-ranking, consistent with their use for pain unresponsive to first-line therapy (NHS, 2022a) and, for dihydrocodeine, short-term relief of dyspnoea (Beng et al., 2021). Their persistently high ranking despite small shifts in percentage share indicates ongoing reliance on opioid analgesics in primary care despite opioid stewardship guidance (Levy, Lord, and Lobo, 2021). Although total analgesics volume remains broadly stable, indicating no major pandemic-related surge in overall demand (Table 1), Figure 1 reveals modest compositional changes. Paracetamol 500mg tablet and co-codamol 30/500mg tablet gain slight shares across the study window, while several other opioid formulations, notably tramadol 50mg tablet, co-dydramol 10/500mg tablet and co-codamol 8/500mg tablet show gradual declines. The downward trends in tramadol and co-dydramol likely reflect national stewardship discouraging prolonged use of these drugs due to dependence risks and limited long-term benefit (UKMI, 2014). The reduced presence of lower-strength co-codamol partly reflects formulation availability: the 8/500mg strength is available over the counter from pharmacies and therefore is under-represented in prescribing datasets, whereas higher strengths (15/500mg and 30/500mg) require a prescription (NHS, 2023). Co-codamol 30/500mg tablets and capsules appear in both Table 1 and Figure 1, with tablets consistently high-volume and capsules lower and slowly declining volumes, reflecting formulation preference rather than differences in clinical indication. Paediatric paracetamol suspensions (120mg/5ml and 250mg/5ml) contribute around 2-2.6% of prescribing with different trajectories (Figure 1). The 250mg/5ml formulation, used in children aged 6 years and above (NHS, 2025), displays a clear trough during the COVID followed by rebound after COVID, consistent with reduced childhood infections with less contact and fewer GP consultations during lockdown, then recovery as social contact resumed. The 120mg/5ml infant formulation (NHS, 2025) shows a more sustained decline, potentially reflecting smaller proportional changes in exposure and illness among infants who already have limited social mixing. Sumatriptan 50mg tablet shows gradual growth, reflecting sustained migraine burden (NHS, 2022b). Codeine phosphate 30mg tablet and morphine oral solution 10mg/5ml appear in the top 10 table only after COVID, suggesting a small rise in community-managed severe pain, breathlessness, or cough in a subset of patients (NHS, 2024; NICE, 2025).

Taken together, Table 1 and Figure 1 indicate a largely stable analgesic formulary with subtle shifts: slightly increased reliance on paracetamol-based regimens, formulation-specific changes within opioid analgesics, symptom-related variation in migraine treatments, age-linked variation in paediatric use, and no evidence of a pandemic-driven surge in total analgesics prescribing.

Figure 1. Change in Share of Union Top10 Analgesics Prescription

## Track changes in share for the union of top-10 drugs and visualise as a heatmap
# Union of Top-10 analgesic codes across all periods
top10_union <- Top10_each_period_data %>%
  distinct(bnf_item_code, bnf_item_description)
# Pull paid_items for union codes from full period data, then compute share
union_shares <- AnalgesicsAllPeriods %>%
  semi_join(top10_union, by = "bnf_item_code") %>%
  left_join(Period_totals_data, by = "period") %>%
  mutate(share = paid_items / total_paid_items) %>%
  select(period, bnf_item_code, bnf_item_description, share) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from  = period, values_from = share, values_fill = 0)
# Contrast the percentage share of the union top-10 drugs before vs during vs after COVID
union_change <- union_shares %>%
  mutate(`During vs Before` = `During COVID` - `Before COVID`,
         `After vs During`  = `After COVID`  - `During COVID`,
         `After vs Before`  = `After COVID`  - `Before COVID`) %>%
  pivot_longer(cols = c(`During vs Before`,`After vs During`,`After vs Before`), names_to = "contrast", values_to = "delta") 
# Order rows by maximum absolute share change across contrasts to highlight drugs with the largest compositional shifts
order_levels <- union_change %>%
  group_by(bnf_item_description) %>%
  summarise(max_abs = max(abs(delta)), .groups="drop") %>%
  arrange(desc(max_abs)) %>%
  pull(bnf_item_description)
# Heatmap
union_change_plot <- union_change %>% 
  mutate(contrast = factor(contrast, levels = c("During vs Before", "After vs During", "After vs Before")), bnf_item_description = factor(bnf_item_description, levels = rev(order_levels)), drug_label = str_wrap(bnf_item_description, width = 28))
# Symmetric colour limits around 0
lim <- max(abs(union_change_plot$delta), na.rm = TRUE)
ggplot(union_change_plot, aes(x = contrast, y = drug_label, fill = delta)) +
  geom_tile(color = "white", linewidth = 0.6) +
  geom_text(aes(label = percent(delta, accuracy = 0.1)), size = 3.2, fontface = "bold") +
  scale_fill_gradient2(low = "#2166ac", mid = "white", high = "#b2182b", limits = c(-lim, lim), breaks = pretty_breaks(5), labels = percent_format(accuracy = 0.1), name = "Δ share")+
  scale_x_discrete(position = "top") +
  labs(title = "Figure 1. Change in Share of Union Top10 Analgesics Prescription", subtitle = "Union of Top-10 drugs across periods (values are percentage_point changes)", x = NULL, y = NULL, caption = "Warm colours = increased share; Cool colours = decreased share.") + theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(panel.grid = element_blank(), axis.text.x = element_text(face = "bold", size = 10), axis.text.y = element_text(size = 9), plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", size = 15, hjust = 0.5), plot.subtitle = element_text(size = 10, hjust = 0.5, margin = margin(b=8)), legend.title = element_text(face = "bold"), legend.key.height = unit(1.1, "cm"), plot.margin = margin(10, 12, 10, 10))

Figure 2. Monthly Presciption of All Analgesics in Scotland

To assess whether changes in drug share reflected redistribution within a stable total volume or shifts in overall analgesic use, Figure 2 presents monthly totals for all BNF 04.07 items from before COVID to after COVID, with the COVID restriction period shaded.

The series shows a pronounced peak around March 2020 (i.e. start of COVID-19), followed by a temporary reduction during the restriction period and a steady recovery from 2022 onwards, reaching slightly higher levels than in the pre-pandemic years. The short-lived surge at the onset of COVID-19 may reflect anticipatory stockpiling. The mid-pandemic dip aligns with reduced primary-care contact and fewer non-COVID illnesses. The subsequent rebound and mild overshoot may reflect catch-up of deferred care and ongoing pain needs in patients whose treatment or procedures were delayed.

Figure 3. Monthly Prescription of Union Top10 Analgesics

Building on the aggregate pattern in Figure 2, Figure 3 examines monthly trajectories for individual top-10 analgesics to identify which drugs contributed to the stable total volume and which diverged.

Paracetamol 500mg tablet, co-codamol 30/500mg tablet, and 15/500mg tablet show pronounced spikes at the start of COVID-19 followed by higher post-pandemic baselines, consistent with their rising shares in Figure 1. Tramadol 50mg capsule, co-codamol 30/500mg capsule, co-dydramol 10/500mg tablet, and co-codamol 8/500mg tablet display steady long-term declines, aligning with the downward patterns in Table 1 and Figure 1. Paediatric paracetamol suspensions exhibit month-to-month variabilities but a shared trough during COVID restriction period, with subsequent recovery more evident in the 250 mg/5ml formulation used in older children. Codeine phosphate 30mg tablet, dihydrocodeine 30mg tablet, sumatriptan 50mg tablet and morphine oral solution 10mg/5ml all show gradual upward trends, again reflecting patterns seen in Table 1 and Figure 1.

Taken together, these drug-specific trajectories indicate that the brief pandemic-related disturbance rapidly stabilised, and that longer-term changes reflect a gradual redistribution within the analgesic formulary rather than shifts in total demand.

Conclusion

Overall, Scottish community analgesic prescribing remained highly stable across the pre-, during-, and post-COVID periods, with no pandemic-driven surge in total volume. The main shifts were compositional: paracetamol 500mg and co-codamol 30/500mg tablets remained dominant with slight post-COVID increases; several opioid formulations declined; and selected agents such as sumatriptan, dihydrocodeine, codeine phosphate, morphine oral solution showed modest growth. Paediatric formulations displayed age-specific disruption and recovery. These findings indicate a largely steady formulary with subtle redistribution among drug classes rather than major COVID-induced changes in overall analgesic demand.

However, interpretation is limited by several factors. Paid-item counts measure dispensing events only and do not capture dose, treatment duration, indication, or over-the-counter and hospital supply. The analysis uses aggregate data and focuses on the union of the top-10 drugs, so it cannot identify patient-level variation by age, deprivation, or diagnosis, nor trends in less commonly prescribed agents. Broad period definitions may also obscure short-term policy phases or regional differences, and the descriptive design cannot establish causality.

Future work should incorporate dose-based metrics and link prescribing records to demographic and clinical datasets to explore subgroup differences and policy effects. Extending the time horizon and applying formal time-series methods would clarify the persistence and drivers of these trends, and the same workflow could be adapted to monitor other therapeutic areas.
